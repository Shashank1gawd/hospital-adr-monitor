Why hospitals report ADRs

Clinical trials are small and short. Many reactions only appear once thousands of ordinary patients use a medicine. Hospital reports are the front line of spontaneous reporting: they reach the national centre (PvPI in India), which feeds the global WHO database.

What should be reported?

Any suspected reaction, especially serious, unexpected, or involving new medicines, children, pregnancy or older adults. You do not need to be sure the drug caused it. A suspicion is enough.

WHO-UMC causality

Categories: Certain, Probable, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional and Unassessable. It looks at timing, other explanations, response to stopping the drug, how typical the reaction is, and rechallenge.

Severity vs seriousness

Seriousness uses regulatory criteria such as hospitalisation or death. Severity (Hartwig and Siegel) describes the impact on treatment and care, from mild to severe.

Preventability

Schumock and Thornton asks whether the reaction could have been avoided, for example a known allergy, wrong dose, or missed monitoring. This drives medication-safety improvements on the ward.

Dechallenge and rechallenge

Dechallenge: did the reaction improve after stopping the drug? Rechallenge: did it return on restarting? Rechallenge is rarely done deliberately because it can be dangerous.

Repeat-report alerts

The dashboard flags the same drug and reaction reported repeatedly. In real life such clusters prompt a closer look and can become a signal, the topic of Project 1.
